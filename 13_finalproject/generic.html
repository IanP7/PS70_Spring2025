Final Project

Final Project WOOOOOOO

In starting to plan for the final project, the project that I am leaning towards is heated clothing, specifically a thin, wearable vest that has heating elements on it. This would be built such that I could wear it underneath any of my existing jackets, and it would be easily washable if need be. With that in mind, here is the bill of materials that I came up with:
  1. Arguably the most crucial aspect of the heated vest is the heating element. I think that the best and cheapest way to make this happen would be to use positive temperature coefficient (PTC) heating pads. They are quick to warm up and they are able to self regulate their own temperatures to avoid overheating and causing a fire under my jacket. I also want them to cover a decent amount of area. I was thinking about using 4 or 6 (see battery discussion) of these heating pads
  2. I also will need a battery to power these heating elements, which seems like it will be the most challenging and expensive item. I would like to get at least 4-5 hours of battery life from this vest, and ideally I would like something closer to 6-8 hours of battery life for the full days that I will spend working outside in the cold. The easiest voltage to work with is 5V, as that is what both the microcontroller and the heating pads run on, but other voltages are viable with step-up/step-down converters. If I have 4 pads, each of which are drawing 600mA, I need the battery to be rated to at least 2.4A, but I would prefer if I had some more headroom, so I looked for 5V 3A batteries. Based on my (potentially incorrect) math, just powering the 4 heating pads wired in parallel and assuming no loss due to the rest of the electronics, a 10,000mAh battery gives me ~4 hours of battery life. Realistically, this would be less, so I want a higher capacity battery. A 20,000mAh battery would likely give me 6-7 hours of real battery life, so that is what I was looking at. I saw some batteries like this one that fit these requirements, but seem a little sketchy. However, I could pull some clever wiring shenanigans and get 6 heating pads, which would provide more warmth, and potentially use a less sketchy battery. If I wired 3 pairs of heating pads in parallel, with each pair wired in series, I would draw 10V and 1.8A of current, which is a (somewhat) compatible demand for lots of power banks that exist already for phones and laptops. I found this one, which is capable of outputting either 9V or 12V with USB PD and can handle 1.8A. It is a little big, but I think that this is the best way of handling power for this project, and it will be able to use 6 heating pads to keep me even warmer. It also is nice because with multiple ports, I can plug the microcontroller into a 5V port directly while also having 9V going to the pads.
  3. USB PD Trigger/Decoder board to get 9V out of the power bank (pads will run at 4.5V instead of 5V, but with more pads this should be fine) like this one
  4. JST connectors for modular pad connections for easy washing
  5. ESP32 or Xiao for the brains of the operation
  6. Power switch (rocker switch) and indicator LED
  7. Rotary encoder for temperature control
  8. Small screen to display battery level and temperature
  9. Temperature sensor (NTC thermistor maybe?)
  10. 22-24 AWG wire
  11. USB A to microUSB cable
  12. Polyester or neoprene to make the vest itself
  13. Waterproof material to make a pouch to house the microcontroller
  14. Velcro or snaps to make things easily removable for washing

Once all is said and done, I hope for the project to look something like this. In this model, the red patches are the heat pads (to scale), the silver box is the battery (to scale), the blue box is the control box that will house the microcontroller, temperature controls, and the display (approximate scale), and the grey dot is the thermistor (approximate scale).

Integrated Project

My working on the integrated project consisted of two parts - designing a PCB and learning to sow. The first part, designing a PCB was a slow-going process, but EasyEDA made the process relatively straightforward. The biggest challenge in this part was designing electronics without ever being able to actually test them - just hoping that they work. I did some preliminary breadboard testing to make sure that all of my theories were correct, but in the end it was essentially just scaling up my MVP to have three heat pads and work on two different power circuits. I have designed one other PCB before, but that one came with a mostly made gerber file already and just required some tweaks, so starting totally from scratch was tough. It took many hours of staring at datasheets from random websites and using the worst search function know to mankind on the LCSC website to find the parts that I was looking for, but once I did, I was able to plop them into the schematic. I connected the heat pads to a circuit that will use 12V input power, though I later realized that I could've streamlined this process since the PD trigger boards are female connectors and not male ones, so it would be board-side and not battery-side, so there is a space for a JST input connector on the board, but I will have to mount the trigger board separately. The LED and rotary encoder work on 5V power, and I added a small OLED screen mount so that I can easily display statistics like temperature and goal temperature. The roatary encoder will be harvested from a board that we already have in the lab, and the transistors are parts that we already have, so I only had the resistors surface mounted.

After I finished the schematic, I moved to PCB design, with the goal of getting it to be as small as possible. I also wanted it to be narrow to minimize the footprint and weight distribution effects that this would have on the vest. The Autoroute option in EasyEDA was completely useless, so I went at it myself and manually routed wires. I also needed multiple thicknesses of wire because some wires would carry 12V, 2A and some would carry 5V, 1A. This process almost felt like a puzzle game to me, trying to ensure that everything go to where I wanted it to without crossing any other wires, so that part was pretty fun I guess. I also added pin holes for the last available analog pin on the Xiao that wasn't occupied, and added multiple additional busses for I2C connectors, as I realized that I did not have any remaining pins if I wanted more than one temperature sensor. With the I2C option, I can hook up multiple compatible sensors to the same 4 pins that are already being used by the OLED screen. I haven't figured out exactly how I'm going to do this yet, so I just gave myself the option with some additional pin holes. I also added generic through holes for ease of mounting. Finally, I went crazy with the silkscreen labelling to make my life as easy as possible determining what goes where to minimize future multimeter guess and check work. After I was happy with my design, I went through the ordering steps and sent all of my preferences and files to Nathan for ordering. No particular options were selected - I wanted to have a purple PCB, but it would've taken longer, and no one is going to see this PCB ever, so it didn't matter.

After the PCB was done, I got to actually working on the packaging of this project. The main target that I wanted to tackle for this week was making some semblance of the vest and learning how to sow in the process. I have used a sewing machine once or twice before, but it was always just super basic straight stitches by pressing the pedal down and letting it do its thing. This time, I would need to do a bit more than that. The freecycle in my house is always literally overflowing, so my plan was to go rifle through those bins and find a very oversized shirt or something that would work for the midlayer goal of my vest - I want it to be able to fit over top of a sweater or bulky shirt but not be too baggy. I was out of town over the weekend and spent all of the time allotted to PS70 the week before on the cinnabot, so that meant that I left this process until Monday. Of course, the one and only time the freecycle has LITERALLY EVER THIS ENTIRE YEAR been cleaned out was over the weekend, leaving my original plan high and dry. I went back to the drawing board and pulled out these black undershirts that I have, which I think could work well since they are thin, easily washable, and stretchy, allowing them to fit comfortably over various thicknesses. Unfortunately, since they are undershirts, they were way too tight. Because of this, I got to tackling my first large sewing project - combining the two tight undershirts into one, looser and baggier undershirt. I cut the sides off of one to the desired thickness, split the sides of the other that would eventually form the midlayer, and much like Dr. Frankenstein did to his monster, sewed the pieces all together into a larger version of what once was.

Considering this was my first time doing anything even remotely similar to this, I was pretty happy with how it turned out - it's amazing what you can learn from YouTube. I had expanded the midlayer to be a pretty ideal width. The width was way too tight and now it fits overtop of bulky shirts and sweaters well. Unfortunately, it still looks a little dumb because the armpits are the portmanteau of two sweaters, and the length is way too long. I tried to address these issues last night, but I was way too tired from my weekend and was butchering it. I also realized that I needed some more precision, so I went back to my dorm and asked for some help pinning the midlayer to better dimensions. Now, the midlayer is all pinned to the right dimensions including the armpits and the length, so the next steps will be sewing those dimensions into place, sewing the pockets on, and sewing the zipper on!
